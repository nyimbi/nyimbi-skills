# Structure Models

Pick one. Mixing maps produces a draft that satisfies two sets of expectations and
neither story.

## Three-act (default for features and shorts)

| Act | Function | Ends on |
|---|---|---|
| I | Establish the world, the want, and the terms of normal | First plot point: the protagonist commits |
| II | Escalation; the want costs more than expected | Midpoint reversal; all-is-lost / dark night |
| III | The protagonist chooses using what they learned | Resolution; new normal |

- Act I ≈ 20–25%, Act II ≈ 50–60%, Act III ≈ 20–25%.
- The **first plot point** is a choice, not an event that happens to the protagonist.
- The **midpoint** changes the goal or the stakes, not just the intensity.
- The **all-is-lost** moment strips the false belief; the finale tests the new one.

## Eight-sequence (features)

Eight sequences of roughly 10–15 minutes. Each sequence has its own question, pressure,
and turn, and ends in a state the next sequence must deal with.

1. Ordinary world and the disturbance
2. The new situation and its rules
3. The first real attempt and its cost
4. The midpoint: a win that changes the terms
5. The consequence of the win
6. The collapse
7. The choice
8. The finale and the new normal

Useful when a feature sags in Act II: a sequence is a film in miniature, so a flat
sequence is easy to find and fix.

## Story circle (Dan Harmon)

Comfort → Want → Go → Adapt → Get → Pay → Return → Changed.

Best for character-driven stories where the world is ordinary and the pressure is
internal. The "pay" is the price of getting what was wanted; the "changed" is the
difference between the opening and closing image.

## Kishōtenketsu

Introduction (ki) → development (shō) → twist (ten) → reconciliation (ketsu).

No antagonist required. The twist recontextualizes the first two movements; the
reconciliation shows the new whole. Strong for shorts, folktales, and endings that
refuse a conventional confrontation.

## Short-film shape (5–15 minutes)

One protagonist. One problem. One turn. One final image.

- **20% setup** — enough to want something.
- **50% pressure** — the attempts, each raising the cost.
- **20% turn** — the choice or revelation that makes the ending inevitable.
- **10% release** — the final image, held.

A short should be a **moment**, not a compressed feature. Cut subplots, not scenes.

## Episode shape (5–10 minutes)

| Part | Function |
|---|---|
| Cold open / hook | A question, image, or threat in the first 15–30 s |
| Act break 1 | The problem is bigger or more personal than assumed |
| Act break 2 | The plan fails, or a relationship breaks |
| Button / cliffhanger | A new question that earns the next episode |

Every episode needs a **self-contained promise** and a **season-level turn**. An episode
that only advances the season is a chapter; an episode that only resolves its own problem
is filler.

## Vertical micro-drama (60–180 seconds)

- **0–3 s:** a visual or verbal hook that makes stopping impossible.
- **Every 15–20 s:** a new escalation, reversal, or reveal.
- **Last 5 s:** a cliffhanger or a satisfying button.
- Design for 9:16 natively: faces in the upper middle, action out of the bottom 20%,
  captions planned from the start.

## Choosing under AI-production constraints

- Few locations → three-act or short-film shape; sequence boundaries can be locations.
- One lead and a small cast → story circle or short-film shape.
- Twist-led premise → kishōtenketsu.
- Serialized hooks → episode shape, with the season grid written first.
- Feature on a local budget → eight-sequence, so each sequence can be produced and
  reviewed as a unit.
