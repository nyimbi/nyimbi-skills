# Customer, Value Proposition and Positioning

## Persona as a job, not a demographic

A persona earns its place only if it changes a product decision. Write one page per priority
segment:

```markdown
# <Segment name>

- **Job:** the progress they are trying to make, in their words.
- **Current alternative:** what they do today, including nothing and spreadsheets.
- **Trigger:** the event that makes them look this week.
- **Pains:** expensive, slow, risky, embarrassing, unreliable.
- **Gains:** what a good outcome looks like, measured.
- **Buying process:** who feels it, who approves, who blocks.
- **Proof:** what convinces them; what makes them distrust you.
- **Watering holes:** where they already gather.
```

Do not average two segments. If the product serves both, write two pages and say which one
the first release serves.

## Value proposition

Write three to five promises. Each has:

1. The customer.
2. The outcome, in their language.
3. The proof: a number, a demo, a guarantee, a reference.
4. The alternative it replaces.

Test: "Compared with <current alternative>, <product> gives <customer> <outcome> because
<proof>."

Reject promises that are adjectives ("seamless", "powerful", "delightful") without a
measurable consequence.

## April Dunford positioning

| Component | What to write |
|---|---|
| Competitive alternatives | What would they use if you did not exist? Include the status quo. |
| Unique attributes | Features or properties the alternatives lack. |
| Value | What those attributes let the customer do, in outcome terms. |
| Who cares | The segment that values that outcome most. |
| Market category | The context that makes the value obvious without explanation. |

Then write the positioning statement:

> For <who cares> who <job>, <product> is a <category> that <value>. Unlike <alternative>, it
> <unique attribute>.

## Messaging hierarchy

- **One-liner:** category + value + who.
- **Three pillars:** each a claim with a proof point.
- **Proof:** demo, metric, customer quote, security or compliance fact.
- **Objection handling:** price, switching cost, trust, "we already have a tool".

## Switch test

Show the positioning and the current alternative to five target users. Ask:

1. What does this replace?
2. What changes for you?
3. Who would care most?
4. What would make you distrust it?

If they cannot answer 1 and 2 without help, the category or the value is wrong.

## Common failures

- Positioning against a named competitor when the real alternative is a spreadsheet.
- A category that requires a paragraph of explanation.
- A "who cares" segment defined by demographics instead of the job.
- Proof points that are features, not outcomes.
- Messaging that changes by channel; the core promise should not.
