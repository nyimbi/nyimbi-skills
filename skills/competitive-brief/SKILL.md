# Competitive Brief

> If you see `~~connector` placeholders, that source is optional — skip the step when it is not available.

Research competitors and generate a structured competitive analysis comparing positioning, messaging, product, content strategy, and market presence — then tie it to a decision.

## Workflow

### 1. Scope the Analysis

Gather from the user:

1. **Competitor(s)** — one or more specific competitors, or a feature area to compare across competitors (required)
2. **Focus** — full product comparison, specific feature area, pricing/packaging, go-to-market, or positioning (if unspecified, cover all)
3. **Your company context** (optional but recommended) — what you sell and to whom, your positioning/value proposition, key differentiators to highlight
4. **Decision context** — what will this inform? Product strategy, sales enablement, investor/board materials, feature prioritization, or positioning

### 2. Research

**Via web search**, for each competitor:
- Product pages and feature lists; pricing pages and packaging
- Homepage messaging, about page, recent news (press releases, funding, launches, partnerships — last 6 months)
- Recent product launches, blog posts, and changelogs
- Content strategy: blog topics, resource types, webinars, podcasts, social presence
- Review sites and third-party comparisons (G2, Capterra, TrustRadius, Product Hunt); analyst reports (Gartner, Forrester, IDC)
- Customer review themes and ratings
- Job postings — hiring signals that indicate strategic direction
- Financial filings for public companies (revenue, growth, investment areas)

**Primary sources (direct from competitor):** website, blog/resource center, social profiles, product demos and free trials (UX, features, onboarding), webinars and events, press room, careers page.

**Secondary sources (third-party):** review sites, analyst reports, news coverage, social listening (mentions, sentiment, share of voice), SEO tools (keyword rankings, traffic estimates, content gaps), community forums (Reddit, Discourse, industry Slack groups).

If **~~knowledge base** is connected: search for existing competitive analysis documents, win/loss reports, sales battle cards, and prior research. If **~~chat** is connected: search for competitive mentions in sales or product channels and recent deal feedback.

**Cadence:** deep analysis quarterly (all sources); monitoring monthly (announcements, content, messaging changes); real-time alerts ongoing (brand mentions, press, job postings).

### 3. Generate the Brief

Use the **Brief Structure** below. Include your company only where the user provided their positioning context.

### 4. Follow Up

After the brief, offer:
- Create a battlecard for the sales team based on this analysis
- Draft messaging that exploits the positioning gaps identified
- A one-page executive summary
- A "how to win against [competitor]" guide
- A competitive monitoring plan
- Deeper analysis on any specific competitor

## Brief Structure

### 1. Executive Summary
- 2-3 sentence overview of the competitive landscape
- Key takeaway: your biggest opportunity and biggest threat

### 2. Competitive Landscape

**Identify the competitive set at multiple levels:**
- **Direct**: same problem, same users, same way — the products your customers actively evaluate against you
- **Indirect**: same problem, different approach (e.g., spreadsheets vs a dedicated tool); include non-consumption — doing nothing or using a manual process
- **Adjacent**: don't compete today but could — similar technology, customer base, or distribution; platforms that could add your functionality as a feature
- **Substitutes**: entirely different ways to solve the underlying need — hiring a person, a general-purpose tool, outsourcing

**Landscape map**: position competitors on the two dimensions that best reveal strategic differences for your market. Common axes: breadth vs depth (suite vs point solution); SMB vs enterprise; self-serve vs sales-led; simple vs powerful; horizontal vs vertical; price vs capability; ease of use vs power; innovative vs established.

### 3. Competitor Profiles

For each competitor:

**Company overview**
- What they do (one-sentence positioning)
- Target audience; company size/stage indicators (funding, headcount if available)
- Founding, size, funding/revenue if public; target market
- Recent momentum: launches, funding, partnerships, customer wins

**Messaging analysis**
- Primary tagline or headline; core value proposition
- Key messaging themes (3-5); tone and voice characterization
- How they describe the problem they solve

**Product/solution positioning**
- How they categorize their product; key features they emphasize
- Claimed differentiators; pricing approach (if publicly available)

**Content strategy**
- Blog frequency and topics; content types produced (ebooks, webinars, case studies, tools)
- Social presence and engagement approach; thought leadership themes
- SEO observations (what terms they appear to target)

**Strengths** — what they do well; where their messaging resonates; genuine competitive advantages; what customers praise
**Weaknesses** — gaps in messaging or positioning; areas where they are vulnerable; consistent customer complaint themes

Be honest and evidence-based — do not dismiss competitors or inflate their weaknesses.

### 4. Feature Comparison

Compare capabilities across the areas relevant to the analysis:

1. **Define capability areas** — group features into categories buyers use when evaluating (not your internal architecture)
2. **List specific capabilities** under each area
3. **Rate each competitor** on a consistent scale

Rating scale — simple (recommended): **Strong** (market-leading, deep, well-executed) · **Adequate** (functional, not differentiated) · **Weak** (exists but limited) · **Absent**. Detailed scale for deep dives: 5 best-in-class · 4 strong · 3 adequate · 2 limited · 1 minimal · 0 absent.

```
| Capability Area | Our Product | Competitor A | Competitor B |
|----------------|-------------|-------------|-------------|
| [Area 1]       |             |             |             |
|   [Feature 1]  | Strong      | Adequate    | Absent      |
|   [Feature 2]  | Adequate    | Strong      | Weak        |
```

Tips: rate from real product experience, customer feedback, and reviews — not marketing claims; "how well do they do X" beats "has feature X"; weight by what matters to your target customers, not total feature count; note pricing caveats (packaging, usage vs seat, custom enterprise pricing); include "why it matters" per capability area; note the date — comparisons go stale fast.

### 5. Positioning and Messaging

**Positioning statement** — for your company and each competitor, extract or reverse-engineer:

> For [target customer] who [need/problem], [Product] is the [category] that [key benefit]. Unlike [competitor/alternative], [Product] [key differentiator].

Sources: homepage headline/subheadline, product descriptions, sales decks, analyst briefings, earnings call language.

**Message architecture** — how each competitor communicates value:
- **Level 1 Category** — what category they claim (CRM, project management, collaboration platform)
- **Level 2 Differentiator** — what makes them different within it (AI-powered, all-in-one, developer-first)
- **Level 3 Value proposition** — the outcome they promise (close deals faster, ship faster)
- **Level 4 Proof points** — customer logos, metrics, awards, case studies

**Value proposition comparison** — for each competitor document: **Promise** (what they promise the customer achieves), **Evidence** (how they prove it), **Mechanism** (how the product delivers), **Uniqueness** (what only they claim to do).

**Narrative analysis** — identify each competitor's story arc: **Villain** (status quo, legacy tools, complexity), **Hero** (the customer? the product?), **Transformation** (the before/after promised), **Stakes** (what happens if you don't act). This reveals positioning strategy and emotional appeals.

**Messaging strengths and vulnerabilities** — assess each competitor's messaging on: clarity (5-second test), differentiation (distinct or generic), proof (claims backed by evidence), consistency (across channels), resonance (real customer pain points).

**Comparison matrix:**

| Dimension | Your Company | Competitor A | Competitor B |
|-----------|-------------|--------------|--------------|
| Primary tagline | ... | ... | ... |
| Target buyer | ... | ... | ... |
| Key differentiator | ... | ... | ... |
| Tone/voice | ... | ... | ... |
| Core value prop | ... | ... | ... |

**Positioning gaps and opportunities** — look for: **unclaimed positions** (value propositions no competitor owns that matter to buyers), **crowded positions** (claims everyone makes that have lost meaning), **emerging positions** (driven by market changes: AI, remote work, compliance), **vulnerable positions** (claims competitors cannot fully deliver on).

### 6. Content Gap Analysis

| Topic/Theme | Your Content | Competitor A | Competitor B | Gap? |
|-------------|-------------|--------------|--------------|------|
| [Topic 1] | Blog post, ebook | Blog series, webinar | Nothing | Opportunity for B |
| [Topic 2] | Nothing | Whitepaper | Blog post, video | Gap for you |

Content type coverage: blog posts, case studies, ebooks/whitepapers, webinars, podcasts, video, interactive tools, templates/resources — map Y/N per player.

Identify opportunities: topics they cover that you do not (gaps to fill); topics you cover that they do not (differentiators to amplify); formats they use that you do not; audience segments they address that you do not; search terms they rank for that you do not (SEO content gaps).

Quality assessment per competitor: depth (surface or comprehensive), freshness (updated or stale), engagement (comments, shares, links), production value, thought leadership (original insights or rehashed).

### 7. Win/Loss Analysis

**Data sources**: CRM notes (immediate, biased), customer interviews shortly after decision (most valuable, least biased), churn/exit interviews, prospect surveys for lost deals.

**Win interview questions:** what problem were you solving? What alternatives did you evaluate? Why us? What almost made you choose someone else? What would make you reconsider?
**Loss interview questions:** what problem were you solving? What did you choose and why? Where did we fall short? What could we have done differently? Would you reconsider, under what conditions?

**Analysis**: track win/loss reasons over time and segment by deal type (enterprise vs SMB, new vs expansion, vertical); identify the top 3-5 reasons for wins and losses; separate product reasons (features, quality) from non-product reasons (pricing, brand, relationship, timing); calculate competitive win rate by competitor.

**Common patterns:** feature gap (a dealbreaker capability); integration advantage (fits tools the buyer already uses); pricing structure (different model, not just cheaper); incumbent advantage (switching costs); sales execution (better demo, faster response, relevant case studies); brand/trust (the safer, well-known option).

### 8. Opportunities and Threats

**Opportunities:** positioning gaps you can exploit; messaging angles no competitor has claimed; audience segments they underserve; content or channel opportunities; capabilities customers ask for that no one provides well; competitor bets you disagree with; market shifts that advantage your approach.

**Threats:** areas where they are strong and you are vulnerable; trends favoring their positioning; recent moves that could shift the market; where they are investing heavily; what a "nightmare scenario" competitive move looks like; where you are most vulnerable.

### 9. Market Trends

**Sources**: analyst reports (market sizing, trends); venture funding themes; conference themes; technology shifts enabling new categories; regulatory changes; customer behavior changes (mobile-first, AI-assisted, privacy-conscious); talent movement.

**Framework per trend:** what is changing (specifically)? Why now (technology, regulation, behavior, economics)? Who is affected? Timeline (now, 1-2 years, 3-5 years)? Implication for us? What are competitors doing?

**Signal vs noise:** signals are trends backed by behavioral data, growing investment, regulatory action, or customer demand; noise is media hype, conference buzz, or announcements without traction. Test against your own customers. Beware "trend of the year" hype cycles.

**Strategic response options:** **Lead** (invest early, define the category — high risk/reward) · **Fast follow** (wait for demand signals, move quickly) · **Monitor** (track, set triggers to act) · **Ignore** (explicitly document why it is not relevant).

### 10. Strategic Implications and Recommended Actions

Tie the analysis back to the decision:
- What should we build, accelerate, or deprioritize?
- Where should we differentiate vs achieve parity?
- How should we adjust positioning or messaging?
- What should we monitor going forward?

Then 3-5 specific, actionable recommendations, split:
- **Quick wins** — actable this week
- **Strategic moves** — longer-term positioning or content investments

## Battlecard

A one-page reference for sales and marketing teams:

- **Header** — competitor name/logo, last updated, competitive win rate (if tracked)
- **Quick overview** — what they do (one sentence), their target customer, pricing model summary, key recent developments
- **Their pitch** — how they describe themselves, primary tagline, top 3 claimed differentiators
- **Strengths (be honest)** — where they genuinely compete well, what customers like, capabilities where they lead
- **Weaknesses** — consistent customer complaints, technical limitations, offering gaps
- **Our differentiators** — 3-5 specific ways you differ; for each: the differentiator, why it matters to the customer, and proof
- **Objection handling:**

| If the prospect says... | Respond with... |
|------------------------|----------------|
| "[Competitor] does X too" | "Here is how our approach differs..." |
| "[Competitor] is cheaper" | "Here is what that price difference gets you..." |
| "I've heard good things about [Competitor]" | "They are strong at X. Where we differ is..." |

- **Landmines to set** — questions to ask early that highlight your advantages: "How do you currently handle [area where they are weak]?" "How important is [capability they lack]?" "Have you considered [risk your product mitigates]?"
- **Landmines to defuse** — questions competitors may encourage prospects to ask you, with prepared responses
- **Win/loss themes** — common reasons deals are won against them / lost to them; which prospect types favor them vs you
- **Maintenance** — review at least quarterly; update immediately after major competitor announcements; incorporate win/loss feedback; track which objection responses work best

## Positioning Strategy

**Category strategy:**
- **Create a new category** — if you do something genuinely different, define and own it (high risk, high reward)
- **Reframe the existing category** — change how buyers evaluate it to favor your strengths
- **Win the existing category** — compete directly on recognized criteria and out-execute
- **Niche within the category** — own a specific segment, use case, or audience

**2x2 positioning map** — plot yourself and competitors on the two most important dimensions for your market; identify the underserved quadrant or where your differentiation is strongest.

**Pitfalls to avoid:** positioning against a competitor rather than for a customer need; claiming too many differentiators (pick 1-2 that matter most); category jargon the customer does not use; positioning on features rather than outcomes; changing positioning too frequently.

## Output Format

Present the full competitive brief with clear formatting; tables for feature comparisons; clear headers per section. Keep the strategic implications section concise and actionable — that is where the value is. Note the research date so the user knows the freshness of the data, and flag areas that change quickly.

## Tips

- Be honest about competitor strengths. A comparison that always shows you winning is not credible.
- Focus on what matters to customers, not to product teams — customers do not care about architecture elegance.
- Pricing is hard to compare fairly — always note the caveats.
- Job postings are underrated intelligence. A competitor hiring ML engineers signals a strategic direction.
- Customer reviews are gold — what real users love and hate, unfiltered by marketing.
- The most valuable part is the "so what" — the strategic implications. Do not skip this.
- Competitive analysis has a shelf life — note the date and refresh cadence.
